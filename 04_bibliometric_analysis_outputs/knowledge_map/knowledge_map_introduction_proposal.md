### 2. Why this literature is difficult to read as a coherent field

**Subsection title:** Conceptual and Methodological Fragmentation in Competency Measurement

**Key points to develop:**
- The construct of "competency" remains contested across educational psychology, engineering education, and psychometric traditions, with no universally accepted taxonomy
- Transversal competencies (e.g., teamwork, ethical reasoning, critical thinking) are operationalized differently across studies, making cross-study comparison problematic
- Measurement instruments range from self-report scales and rubrics to performance-based assessments and situational judgment tests, each with distinct validity assumptions
- Disciplinary silos within engineering (mechanical, electrical, civil, software) produce domain-specific instruments that rarely transfer across specializations
- Cultural and linguistic adaptations of instruments introduce additional variability, yet adaptation processes are inconsistently reported
- The field lacks a shared framework for evaluating measurement properties (e.g., COSMIN standards are rarely applied in engineering education research)

**Relationship to the study:** Frames the conceptual complexity that a systematic review must address. This fragmentation justifies the need for a structured synthesis that maps instruments, their properties, and their contexts of use. The subsection does not depend on corpus findings.

### 3. Why existing evidence still lacks integrative synthesis

**Subsection title:** Gaps in Prior Reviews and Synthesis Efforts

**Key points to develop:**
- Existing reviews of competency measurement in engineering education have focused primarily on specific competency domains (e.g., teamwork, sustainability, ethics) without cross-domain integration
- Prior syntheses tend to emphasize instrument development rather than systematic evaluation of measurement properties (validity, reliability, responsiveness)
- Geographic bias is evident: most instruments originate from North America, Europe, and Australia, with limited evidence from Global South contexts
- Sample sizes in validation studies are often small and institution-specific, limiting generalizability of psychometric evidence
- Previous reviews have not systematically applied established quality criteria for measurement properties (e.g., COSMIN, EMPRO, or adapted frameworks)
- No prior review appears to have combined bibliometric mapping with inductive thematic analysis to simultaneously examine intellectual, social, and conceptual structures of the field
- The absence of triangulated synthesis means that relationships between instrument types, competency domains, and measurement property evidence remain unexplored

**Relationship to the study:** Defines the specific gap that the present review addresses. The gap is constructed from limitations of prior reviews (scope, methodology, geographic coverage) rather than from the present study's findings.

### 4. Why bibliometric mapping with inductive depth is needed now

**Subsection title:** The Case for a Multi-Method Synthesis Approach

**Key points to develop:**
- Bibliometric mapping can reveal the intellectual structure (citation lineages, influential works), social structure (collaboration networks, geographic patterns), and conceptual structure (thematic clusters, keyword evolution) of a research field
- Inductive thematic analysis adds interpretive depth by identifying latent themes, conceptual tensions, and emergent patterns that bibliometric indicators alone cannot capture
- Triangulation across methods (bibliometric + inductive + cross-method comparison) strengthens the validity of synthesized findings and reduces method-specific biases
- The combination of performance indicators (FWCI, citation percentiles) with structural mapping provides a more complete picture of research impact and knowledge diffusion
- This multi-method approach is particularly suited to fragmented fields where no single analytical lens can capture the full complexity of instrument development and validation practices
- The approach allows simultaneous examination of what has been measured (instruments), how well it has been measured (psychometric evidence), and who has done the measuring (collaboration patterns)

**Relationship to the study:** Justifies the methodological choice at a high conceptual level without describing procedural details. Connects the method to the specific challenges of the field identified in subsections 2 and 3.
